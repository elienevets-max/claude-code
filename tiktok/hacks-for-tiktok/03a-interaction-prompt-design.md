# Skill 3A: Interaction Prompt Design

**Domain: Engagement Engineering**

## Skill Definition

Create specific, compelling reasons for viewers to respond, comment, share, or save — designing engagement as intentional behavior, not hoping for spontaneous reaction.

## Why This Skill Matters

The book frames engagement as **designed behavior**. Comments, shares, and saves don't happen because content is good — they happen because the content structure creates a specific psychological impulse to act. A video can be excellent and still generate zero engagement if it doesn't include an interaction trigger. Conversely, a simpler video with a well-designed trigger can generate massive engagement.

## Effective Engagement Triggers

### Ask for Opinions or Choices
- Present two or more options and ask the viewer to choose
- "Which one would you pick: A or B?"
- "Rate this from 1 to 10"
- "Am I right or am I crazy?"
- "Is this a yes or a no?"

**Why it works:** The human brain automatically forms opinions when presented with options. Asking them to express that opinion converts a mental response into a physical action (typing a comment).

**Design principles:**
- The choice must be genuinely debatable — if one option is obviously better, viewers think the answer but don't comment
- Simple choices get more responses (A or B) than complex ones (explain your reasoning)
- Place the question early enough that viewers are thinking about it while watching, not just at the end

### Invite Viewer Participation
- Ask viewers to contribute their own experience, knowledge, or creativity
- "What would you add to this list?"
- "Drop your best tip below"
- "How do YOU handle this?"
- "Show me yours in the comments"

**Why it works:** Participation creates a sense of co-creation. The viewer becomes part of the content, not just a consumer of it.

**Design principles:**
- The invitation must be specific enough to answer easily but open enough to allow personal expression
- "What's your experience?" is too broad. "What's the one tool you can't live without?" is actionable
- Acknowledge contributions by replying or featuring them in future content (rewards participation, encourages more)

### Present Relatable Scenarios
- Describe a situation the target audience has experienced
- "Tell me I'm not the only one who..."
- "That moment when you..."
- "Every [niche professional] knows this feeling"
- "This is for anyone who's ever..."

**Why it works:** Recognition is one of the strongest emotional triggers. When viewers see their experience reflected, they feel compelled to confirm it ("YES, this is so real").

**Design principles:**
- Specificity creates relatability — general scenarios feel generic, specific scenarios feel personal
- The scenario should name something the viewer has felt but hasn't seen articulated
- Relatable scenarios drive both comments ("so true") and shares (tagging friends who relate)

### Pose Unresolved Questions
- Leave a question open that the viewer's brain needs to answer
- "I haven't figured this out yet — has anyone?"
- "Why does [surprising thing] happen?"
- "What am I missing here?"
- Present a problem without a neat solution

**Why it works:** The brain resists unresolved questions. Leaving something open creates psychological tension that the viewer resolves by commenting.

**Design principles:**
- The question must be genuine — audiences detect fake confusion
- Vulnerability (admitting you don't know something) increases trust and response rate
- Unanswered questions also drive rewatches and saves as viewers return to check responses

## Interaction Prompt Placement

### The Three-Point Method
1. **Early prompt (3-5s):** Seed the interaction trigger early so viewers are thinking about it while watching
   - "Let me know in the comments if this has happened to you"
   - Sets up a response frame before the main content
2. **Mid-video prompt (midpoint):** Reinforce or introduce the main interaction opportunity
   - "Okay, which of these two approaches would you use?"
   - The viewer is now invested and more likely to respond
3. **Closing prompt (final 3-5s):** Direct call to action aligned with the content
   - "Comment your answer below"
   - Converts the impulse built during the video into action

### Natural vs. Forced Prompts

| Natural (Effective) | Forced (Ineffective) |
|---|---|
| "Which method works better for you?" | "Like and comment for part 2!" |
| "What would you add to this?" | "Follow me for more!" |
| "Has this happened to anyone else?" | "Drop a [emoji] in the comments!" |
| "Where am I wrong here?" | "Comment 'yes' if you agree!" |

Natural prompts feel like part of the conversation. Forced prompts feel like a transaction.

## Trigger Design by Desired Behavior

| Desired Action | Trigger Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Comments | Opinion/Choice | "A or B? I'm going with A" |
| Shares | Relatability | "Tag someone who does this" |
| Saves | Utility | "Save this for next time you need it" |
| Follows | Curiosity | "I'm doing a series on this — follow for part 2" |
| Rewatches | Density | Pack info too dense for one viewing |

## Skill Benchmark

Every video you post contains at least one specific interaction trigger, and you can predict what type of engagement it will generate before posting.

## Common Failures

- No interaction trigger at all (hoping for engagement without designing for it)
- Generic CTAs that viewers have become blind to ("like and follow!")
- Placing the trigger only at the very end (viewers who drop off early never see it)
- Making the trigger feel separate from the content (bolted on, not integrated)
- Asking for too much effort ("write a paragraph about your experience" — most won't)

## Practice Drill

Before your next 5 videos, write down the specific comment you want to see most. Design an interaction trigger that makes that comment the most natural response. After posting, check whether the comments match your design. Adjust the trigger style based on what actually works for your audience.
