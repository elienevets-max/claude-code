## The TikTok Shop Playbook

### Core Principle: Views > Followers
- **Forget followers.** TikTok shattered the follower model
- Everything is measured in **views** — a brand new account can get 10M views on a single video
- The center of the model is **swings at bat** — volume of content attempts

### The Creator Army Model
Instead of one influencer (the old Instagram/YouTube model), build an **army of creators** making product-specific content:

1. **Recruit many creators** — not celebrities, just hungry people willing to learn
2. **Give them the product** and general angles/hooks
3. **Let them figure out what works** — don't micromanage the creative
4. **Pay commissions through TikTok Shop** — the economics feed themselves
5. **Volume wins** — creators make 30-50 videos/month; most don't work, but a few go nuclear
6. **When one angle works, hundreds copy it** — they make 20% of the original but the brand gets massive reach

### Origin of This Model: Andrew Tate
- Tate didn't build his own TikTok — he had 500+ affiliates making content about him
- Affiliates earned commission per sign-up to his community
- Result: most Googled man on the planet
- This was essentially a **Content MLM** — a self-feeding economic loop
- Streamers like Aiden Ross now spend **six figures/month** paying people on a CPM basis to chop and distribute their content

### How TikTok Shop Makes It Self-Sustaining
- Creators are incentivized by **commission on sales** — no brand has to manage complex payment
- Successful creators realize they can make serious money and scale up to 5+ videos/day
- They start **choosing brands** they think will perform well
- Top creators: **27 people made $100K+ in a single month** in one group alone
- Example: Jacine — was a server making $20/hr, 8 months later making **$180K/month** as a freelance creator

### The Halo Effect / Flywheel
TikTok virality creates a compounding flywheel:
1. Brand goes viral on TikTok → people Google the brand name
2. Increased Google searches → better Google rankings
3. People search on Amazon → better Amazon organic ranking
4. Amazon + Google + DTC all benefit from social virality
5. One channel's spend drives results across all channels

## Anatomy of a Viral TikTok Shop Video

### Case Study: Oil of Oregano Video (~$50K revenue from single video)

**Structure breakdown:**

| Element | What He Did | Marketing Principle |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Hook** | Rode the Trump news trend — "just got into office one day ago" | Newsjacking / riding trending topics |
| **Curiosity Gap** | "going after one of the most predatory industries" — which one? | Information gap keeps viewer watching |
| **Audio** | Mysterious trending background sound | Native to platform, not "ad-like" |
| **Production** | Filming an Android phone screen — raw, unpolished | Doesn't feel like an advertisement |
| **Social Proof** | Mentioned Dana White, famous scientists | Authority stacking |
| **Education** | Built case for oil of oregano benefits before revealing product | Teach, don't sell |
| **Late Product Reveal** | Didn't mention the product until deep into the video | Viewer is invested before the pitch |
| **Scarcity CTA** | "Only bad thing is how fast they sell out" / "orange cart means still in stock" / "flash sale" | Urgency + scarcity close |

### Key Creative Principles

1. **Don't look like an ad** — raw, organic, filmed on phone beats polished production
2. **Hook with something unrelated** — news, conspiracy, trending topic — NOT the product
3. **Create an information gap** — make them need to keep watching to find out
4. **Use trending sounds** — the algorithm rewards native content
5. **Educate before you sell** — build the case, then reveal the product
6. **The product is the punchline** — the story is the content, the product is the resolution
7. **Scarcity close** — "they sell out fast" + visual indicator of availability

### The Craig Clemens Headline Framework (Classic Marketing Applied to TikTok)
The greatest marketing headline ever: *"Wife of famous Hollywood star swears under oath that her new perfume does not contain illegal sexual stimulants"*

Breakdown of elements that translate directly to TikTok hooks:
- **Celebrity association** — curiosity about which star
- **High stakes language** — "swears under oath" = drama, conspiracy, seriousness
- **Forbidden/taboo element** — "illegal sexual stimulants" = so good it should be illegal
- **Implication of extraordinary efficacy** — the product is SO effective people question its legality

> "If a guru in the marketing space doesn't know Craig Clemens, they're not really tapped in. Simple test."

## Brand Case Studies

### 1. Roose Research (NAD / Longevity Supplements)
- **Founder:** 27 years old, little Amazon experience
- **Category bet:** Longevity — predicted it would be the next collagen ($4B exit: Vital Proteins → Nestlé)
- **Path:** Started on Amazon → poured all-in on TikTok when it emerged
- **Result:** ~$15M/month revenue (in a single month), all bootstrapped
- **Brand aesthetic:** Hasn't even revamped the brand yet — "doesn't look like a multi-hundred-million dollar thing"
- **Lesson:** Spot the health trend early enough, build good product, go all-in on TikTok distribution

### 2. Evil Goods (Beef Tallow Skincare)
- **Category bet:** Holistic wellness / anti-toxic skincare — grassroots Twitter movement validated it
- **Angle:** Marketing to **fear** — big pharma, toxic ingredients, endocrine disruptors
- **Name:** Aggressive, polarizing (like Liquid Death — could help or hurt retail later)
- **Breakout video:** Girl with hormonal acne showed before/after — "3 things that helped" — went nuclear
- **One campaign:** 600 million views in a single month for ~$100K spend (pre-Shop era)
- **Key insight:** Whenever you can market to **fear or insecurity** (big pharma, plastics, toxins), it performs on this platform

### 3. Top Shelf (Cologne — "Creed for Gen Z")
- **Revenue:** ~$1M/month
- **Price point:** $90 (not cheap — proves luxury can work on TikTok)
- **Product development:** Hired a "professional nose," tested 8 samples, first one got destroyed in reviews
- **Lesson learned:** First product did $700K in month one but got murdered with bad reviews — Rob didn't love it himself but chased the money instead of making something for himself
- **Recovery:** Revamped with second fragrance that was genuinely good → now the winner
- **Winning hooks:** Female creators calling it "[ ] magnet juice" + boyfriend reaction skits + self-improvement/dating angles
- **Target psychology:** Gen Z male self-improvement, dating, "how do you smell around the girl you're trying to talk to"

### 4. Underbrush Gum
- **Founder:** Scrappy entrepreneur, built his own factory, makes the gum himself
- **Revenue:** Millions per month
- **Key angle:** Rode the "TikTok ban" wave — "my small family business is going to get crushed"
- **Execution:** Founder did ASMR video of making the gum while talking about the ban → creator army picked it up and put their own spin on it
- **Pattern:** Brands go up and down — you need to constantly find new angles. An angle works for ~1 month then fades

## How to Find and Validate Product Opportunities

### The Observation Framework
> "Be an observer, not a consumer on all of these platforms."

- **Hold your opinion** — don't judge if something is "stupid"
- **Be matter-of-fact** — if looks-maxing views are going up month over month, that just IS
- **Work backwards from behavior** — don't ask "should people want this?" Ask "DO people want this?"

### The Twitter/Weird Behavior Signal
From the first product manager at Twitter:

> "Anytime there's a phenomenon of behavior that you see as weird — you don't even understand WHY they're doing it but they undeniably ARE doing it — **lean in** as an entrepreneur. That's your signal. Most people use that same signal to point, laugh, or push away."

**Examples of this pattern:**
- Twitter (2006): "People are texting what they ate for lunch? Stupid." → Became beacon of free speech
- Instagram (2010): "People posting pictures of food? Stupid." → Essential platform
- TikTok (2020): "Kids dancing? Stupid." → Reshaping all of commerce
- Looks-maxing (now): "Guys obsessing over jawlines? Stupid." → Underserved billion-dollar market

> "You're crazy until you're successful, then you're a genius." — MrBeast

### Where to Find Trending Products

1. **Kalodata (kalodata.com)** — directly plugged into TikTok's API
   - Shows top products, top videos, rankings by category
   - Filter by time period (weekly, 30-day)
   - Study what's working right now
2. **Exploding Topics** — identify rising search trends
3. **Amazon data tools** — see what people are searching for (validates demand)
4. **TikTok itself** — observe what content is going viral in product categories

### Product Selection Criteria
- **Consumable** — repeat purchases built in
- **Novel** — something new or differentiated
- **Marketable to fear or insecurity** — big pharma, toxins, plastics, appearance
- **Ties to a cultural movement** — holistic wellness, looks-maxing, self-improvement
- **High margin** — enough room to pay creator commissions and still profit
- **"Somebody's already doing this" = validation**, not a reason to quit — out-execute on marketing

## Critical Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Being Cheap with Creators
- The #1 mistake brand owners make
- This is a **culture-based** market — generosity matters
- Goalie Gummies set the standard: condos, Lamborghinis, Rolexes as creator prizes
- If you offer 20% commission when others offer 25%, creators will leave
- **Be abundant** — "put it out there, it's going to come back"
- Treat creators as partners, not vendors

### 2. Chasing Money Over Product Quality
- Rob's cologne mistake: first product did $700K month one but got destroyed by reviews
- He didn't love it himself but launched anyway because money
- **Make products for yourself** — if you wouldn't use it, don't sell it
- "Making stuff for myself" was the philosophy that built Genius Brand successfully

### 3. Thinking an Angle Lasts Forever
- A winning angle/hook works for about **1 month** then fades
- Brands go up sharply then down — you must constantly find new angles
- The Trump video angle will work for a month, then you need the next one
- Constant creative innovation is required

### 4. Over-Polishing Content
- Raw > polished on TikTok
- Android phone screen recordings outperform studio production
- If it looks like an ad, people scroll past
- The oil of oregano video was intentionally raw — "you don't think this is an advertisement"

### 5. Trying to Build a Brand Page
- "Screw your brand page" — Roose Research doesn't even have a good brand page
- The brand page is not the center of the model
- The center is: how many people can create product-specific content?

### 6. Not Respecting the New Generation
- "Accept that we are a different generation and don't infringe on them"
- Don't impose old-school marketing thinking on TikTok-native creators
- Let go of creative control — "letting go has been part of respecting the future"
- You are likely not the target audience — trust creators who are
