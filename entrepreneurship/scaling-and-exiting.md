## How to Get an Investor

### First: Do You Actually Need One?

Having the wrong investor or investing for the wrong reasons gives you a new boss. It's easy at the beginning when you get the money and gets harder over time.

> "Ask yourself: is there another way to make the business work?"

### Six Ways to Raise Money

#### 1. Family and Friends

**Why it works:** They know you — your personality, dedication, the real you. No convincing needed on character.

**The one rule:** Tell them the truth — they could lose all their money. Don't oversell. If you're clear about the risk, the rest is up to them.

Also consider friend-of-a-friend. A personal recommendation is the most powerful entry point.

#### 2. Your Team as Investors

> "The smart people think: who's the best — and can they also invest?"

Look for the best talent who also has capital. Example: recruit the #2 person at a competitor — they'll never be #1 there, but they could be #1 with you. They likely have money to invest and will take a lower salary for equity.

Simon's team members at HelpBank invested their own money in the business and received equity. Money from people working in the business is far better than money from people externally asking for reports.

#### 3. Angel Investors

**The wrong way:** "Hi, I've got a business, would you like to invest?"

**The right way:** Ask for help, not money.

- Angels want to feel valued, not like an ATM
- If they can't bring value beyond capital, they don't want to invest
- Create FOMO — make it clear that if they don't invest, they're missing out
- Make it slightly harder for people to get involved
- Simon has invested in 78 businesses — he feels "honored and lucky" when founders make him feel special about being involved

> "If you ask me for advice, you're more likely to get money out of me than if you straight up ask me for money."

#### 4. Venture Capital (VCs)

VCs typically want businesses that are proven and need capital to scale. Three things to check:

| Check | Why It Matters |
|-------|---------------|
| **Do they have money to deploy?** | VCs cycle between raising and deploying funds. If they're raising, they'll take the meeting but won't invest. Google their fund status. |
| **What have they invested in before?** | If they invested in your competitor's space, either they're hedging (good — they'll invest in multiple) or they're exclusive (bad — but you can pitch their rival VC) |
| **Can a portfolio company introduce you?** | An intro from a company the VC already invested in is far more powerful than a cold approach. Also doubles as due diligence — ask that founder what the VC is really like. |

> "They don't take applications online despite what their websites say. They take recommendations."

#### 5. Your Clients and Brand Partners

> "Sometimes your client who wants your service will pay for your expansion."

Simon's client wanted his agency to open in the Middle East. He said no. They offered to fund the expansion. The client then had a vested interest in his success — no more annual tenders, no chopping and changing suppliers.

**Caution:** Be careful about conflicts. If one client invests, it may lock out their competitors from working with you.

**Big brands have investment divisions.** Google Ventures invested in a coffee shop (Blue Bottle). It's possible to get Google to invest in your business.

#### 6. Crowdfunding

Often overlooked but powerful — and you don't give away equity (or you can choose to):

| Type | How It Works |
|------|-------------|
| **Pre-sales** (Indiegogo, Kickstarter) | Sell the product before it's made, use that revenue to build it. No equity given away. |
| **Equity crowdfunding** | Sell small equity stakes to many people |
| **Loans** | Community-backed lending |
| **Donations** (GoFundMe) | Support-based, no repayment expected |

> "Often when it comes to raising money, if you look at your business, it's not money you need — you just need a better sales system."

## How to Get Sponsors

### Two Things That Make Sponsors Say Yes

1. **Value Return (ROI)** — What they invest, they get back in trackable form (views, sales, conversions)
2. **Emotional Sale** — The CEO played football at that local club, or grew up in that community, or personally cares about your mission

**The Holy Grail:** Emotional sale + value return together.

### The Brand Understanding Mistake

Simon tried to sell coaster advertising (ads on drink coasters in bars) to a jewelry brand. They laughed — and he lost them as a client entirely. High-end jewelry doesn't want a beer sitting on top of their logo.

> "Understand the brand. Understand what their values are. Understand how they traditionally advertise."

If Visa sponsors the Olympics, don't pitch them your local event — pitch them something linked to the Olympics.

### Don't Forget the People

Brands are made of people. Research who runs the brand. If the person managing sponsorship at GoDaddy once ran a small business themselves, they'll resonate with your mission to help entrepreneurs. Find the champion inside the company.

### Work Through Intermediaries

Two types of agents brands already work with:

| Type | What They Do | Why It's Easier |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Media Buyers** | Buy ad space on behalf of brands. Already have the budget allocated. | Faster decision, existing relationship with brand, they hold the money |
| **Agencies** (McCann, Saatchi, Ogilvy, Leo Burnett) | Create campaigns for brands, looking for placement ideas | Can insert your product into a campaign they're already building |

> "Stop trying to sell to the brand. Sell to the media buyer."

### The Best Hack: Just Use the Product

Use a brand's product naturally. Simon used a Ring doorbell on his staircase — not as a sponsorship deal, but because it was the best product. Ring and Amazon saw it across millions of views and reached out.

> "Lean into a brand relationship. Don't initially think about the money. Delayed gratification in sponsorship is very powerful."

## How to Build a Brand

### Brand = Purpose, Not Logo

> "Take away the logo, take away everything, and you still know it's that brand."

- Nike: supporting athletes
- Apple: supporting creativity
- A brand is the essence of what you're doing, not the visual identity

### Start with Your Personal Brand

You already have one — people talk about you when you're not in the room. The question is whether you're defining it or letting others define it.

**Write down:**
- Your values
- Your non-negotiables
- What you'd say no to
- What you'd say yes to

These personal brand values translate directly into your company brand. Simon's personal values (honesty, openness, authenticity) became HelpBank's brand values.

### Two Models for Scaling Brand

| Model | How It Works | Example | Risk |
|-------|-------------|---------|------|
| **Reference** | Sponsor/partner with people who represent your brand values | Canon + Peter McKinnon, Nike + athletes | If the influencer does something bad, your brand is damaged (Kanye/Adidas) |
| **Leadership** | A person inside the company embodies the brand | Steve Jobs at Apple, Simon Squibb at HelpBank | When that person leaves, the magic may leave too (Apple post-Jobs) |

**Apple's transition:** Steve Jobs (the visionary Maverick) → Tim Cook (the trustworthy data person). Apple deliberately chose someone who represents what matters most now: trust and data privacy.

### The Most Important Brand Rule: Learn to Say No

> "You can spend 30 years building a reputation. It only takes 5 seconds for it to go to sh*t."

- One bad client with a big mouth can damage a decade of brand building
- If your spider senses say someone isn't right, don't take their money
- Your brand is the most valuable asset you'll ever build
- Simon's company sold for a lot of money because of brand value — "They bought the brand, not the business"

## How to Hire, Grow, and Build

### Hiring: Purpose-First

> "If your business has a purpose and the people you hire believe in that purpose, you will never have to manage anybody."

- Hire around purpose, not just skill
- Check their social media — do they actually care about what they claim to care about?
- Ask for references (people don't do this anymore — they should)
- Give them equity — aligned incentives = loyalty, lower turnover, lower stress

### The Equity Argument

> "I would rather give someone equity and have loyalty than keep 100% and have high turnover, high management stress, and a weaker company."

When someone with equity left Simon's company after 4 years, they let him buy it back at a reasonable rate — because the relationship was good. Good people don't hold you hostage.

### Growing: Know Your Destination

Before growing, answer: **Why are you growing?**

- Is it ego?
- Do you want to bring in management so you can work less?
- Do you want to reach more people?

> "It's easier to run a big company than a small company. Far too many entrepreneurs make their business too small."

### Building: From Generalist to Specialist

Early days: everyone does everything (generalist mindset). To scale: people must specialize.

| Stage | Approach |
|-------|----------|
| **Early** | Founder cleans toilets, does pitches, loads the car |
| **Growth** | Build systems, hire specialists for specific functions |
| **Scale** | Replace yourself — there's probably a better CEO out there for the scaled version |

Simon replaced himself as CEO of Fluid 11 years in with someone better suited for that stage.

**Critical:** Don't sit still. Kodak invented the digital camera but wouldn't launch it because it would kill their film business. Disrupt yourself before someone else does.

## How to Fire Someone: The 7-and-8 Rule

### The Three Categories

| Rating | Description | Action |
|--------|-------------|--------|
| **9s and 10s** | They love you, you love them, they're great at their job | Keep them. Give them equity. Protect them. |
| **1s and 2s** | They know they're bad, you know they're bad | They quit or you fire them. It's obvious and natural. |
| **7s and 8s** | Almost good enough. Some days a 9, then slip back to a 6. | **This is where companies die.** |

### Why 7s and 8s Are Dangerous

- You desperately hope they'll become 9s and 10s
- You're afraid of not finding a replacement
- They take up most of your HR conversations
- **If 9s and 10s see that 7s and 8s can coast without consequences, the 9s and 10s leave**

### How to Handle 7s and 8s

1. **Identify them honestly** — if you're talking about someone more than once or twice a week, you probably have a problem
2. **Try to help first** — maybe they're in the wrong role. Move them to a different department; they might become 9s and 10s
3. **If it doesn't work, fire them** — and help them find a new job where they can be a 9 or 10
4. **Line up the replacement before firing** — but don't keep a 7-8 just because "they're better than nobody"

> "When you fire them, they're often grateful — because they can go find a job they actually enjoy."

## How to Go Global

### Why Going Global Reduces Risk

If you're in one market and it crashes, you're fully exposed. Multiple markets = diversified risk.

> "It's easier to run a big company than a small company."

### How to Go Global

1. **Research** — Identify markets where your product/service could work, even if you don't open there yourself
   - Having global potential makes you more attractive to investors
   - A brand partner might fund your expansion

2. **Franchise** — Someone else opens and runs your brand in another market under license
   - Not just McDonald's and Subway — service businesses can franchise too
   - Closest thing to passive income that actually exists

3. **Think big from the start** — Don't trap yourself in one market
   - A business thinking globally is more likely to survive long-term

## How to Get a Mentor

### What You Actually Need

> "What you actually need is not a mentor. What you need is someone who can answer the questions you have."

| What You Think You Need | What You Actually Need |
|------------------------|----------------------|
| A mentor | Answers to specific questions |
| Someone to keep you accountable | A co-founder |
| Help with sales | A salesperson |
| General guidance | An advisor with equity in your business |

### If You Still Want a Mentor

1. **Research what matters to them** — Don't ask a property-hating entrepreneur to mentor your property business
2. **Define what you mean** — "Will you be my mentor?" is an open-ended question no one can commit to. Instead: "Can I have 10 minutes of your time once a week?" Ask a specific question first.
3. **Reframe as "Advisor"** — Advisory board member is more structured, more glamorous for LinkedIn, and easier to formalize with equity
4. **Get a referral** — A warm introduction is the most powerful way to get someone's time
5. **Give value first** — Someone redesigned Simon's website unprompted. Of course he then gave them his time. Don't extract — contribute.

> "If you ask a question, you will get a mentor. If you ask for a mentor, you'll get no reply."

## How Equity Works

### The Control Myth

> "Equity ownership does not equal control."

Most people refuse to drop below 50% because they think they'll lose control. Wrong. You control your company through the **shareholder/operational agreement**, not through percentage ownership.

### Critical Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Why It's Dangerous |
|---------|-------------------|
| 52/48 split with co-founder | The person with 48% psychologically does 48% of the work. Breeds resentment. **Do 50/50.** |
| Selling too much equity early | You won't have enough to get to the finish line when you need more investors |
| Wrong people on the cap table | 2% given to the wrong person can prevent future investors from coming in |
| No alignment on direction | One partner wants to take profits out, the other wants to reinvest — company collapses |

### 50/50 Partnerships

For decision deadlocks, create a board of advisors — a trusted third party both partners agree on.

But the best prevention: **agree on your destination before you start.** Use your mind map. Know where you're going and why.

### Share Options vs. Actual Equity

| Type | What It Is | Risk |
|------|-----------|------|
| **Share options** | A percentage of future value, often tied to stock market listing. No decision-making power. | Company can delist — you're forced to sell at whatever price. Simon experienced this personally. |
| **Actual equity** | Real ownership in the company. If company sells, you get paid out directly. | More powerful, more aligned with actual ownership |

**Simon's preference:** Give team members actual equity, not share options.

### SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)

Used by Y Combinator and others. Allows investment without determining the company's value today — investor gets a discount on the future valuation.

**Benefits:**
- Removes the "what's your company worth?" conversation
- Easier to get investors
- No immediate tax implications
- Great for incentivizing employees

### Reverse Engineering

Know your end goal (IPO, acquisition, lifestyle business) and structure equity from day one to support that path. Companies that don't do this often can't exit because the shareholder structure is too messy to unwind.

## How to Sell Your Business

### The Paradox: Don't Want to Sell

> "The most amount of money I ever got for a company was because I didn't want to sell it."

Mark Zuckerberg was offered $1B by Yahoo and said no. Facebook became worth a trillion. Build a business you love — selling it will accidentally happen.

### Five Exit Strategies

| Strategy | How It Works | Pros/Cons |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| **Partnership → Acquisition** | Work with a company on a project; they realize they should just buy you | Simon sold Fluid to PwC this way. Natural, high value. |
| **Business Brokers/Agents** | Companies that specialize in selling businesses | Useful if you're too busy to sell and grow simultaneously. Do due diligence on the agent. |
| **Merger with Competitor** | Two companies doing the same thing combine | Easiest path, but often not the highest value |
| **Sell Outside Your Industry** | Sell to a company in a different sector | PwC (accounting) paid more for Fluid (creative agency) than Ogilvy (creative agency) would have — because PwC saw unique value |
| **Management Buyout** | Let your leadership team buy the company | Protects the business, rewards the people who built it with you |

### The Key Principle

> "Do not build a business to sell it. Build a business you love. Investors want to invest in that. Build a business you never want to sell — people want to buy a business like that."

## Quick Reference: Core Principles

1. **Build a brand, not a business** — brands get bought; businesses get commoditized
2. **Give equity to your team** — aligned incentives beat management stress every time
3. **Ask for help, not money** — from investors, mentors, everyone
4. **The 7-and-8 rule** — fire the almost-good-enough before you lose the truly excellent
5. **Don't build to sell** — build to love; the exit will come
6. **Equity ≠ control** — the shareholder agreement decides who has control
7. **Go global to reduce risk** — one market makes you vulnerable
8. **Crowdfund before you fundraise** — pre-sell, validate, keep your equity
9. **Do the journalist's job** — lazy PR outreach gets lazy results
10. **Give without take** — value flows back to those who give freely
