# The Side Hustle Playbook: Testing, Copying & Launching at Volume

> "I've started at least 80 businesses. Cumulatively, low hundreds of millions of revenue. Low tens of millions of profit. But the majority have been abandoned or fizzled out or failed. It's just a numbers game." — Chris (The Kerner Office)

Frameworks for starting, testing, and scaling side hustles from someone who has launched 80+ businesses. Extracted from The Diary of a CEO podcast with Steven Bartlett.

---

## Core Mental Models

### 1. The Copying Framework

> "When I see an idea already exists, I'm like, 'Yes! Someone went to the front lines of the battlefield and validated this for me.'"

Most people Google their idea, see it exists, and give up. The opposite response is correct.

**The reverse-engineering process:**
1. Find a competitor doing what you want to do
2. Go to **Web Archive** (web.archive.org) — see snapshots of their website over time
3. Go to **Similar Web** — see their traffic over time
4. Overlay the two — when they redesigned for mobile, did traffic jump? When they changed pricing from $99 to $49/bimonthly, what happened?
5. **Start where they are today** — skip the years of trial and error

| What Most People Think | What Actually Works |
|----------------------|-------------------|
| "I need an original idea" | Copy what's already working |
| "That business already exists, I'm too late" | Existence = validation. The market is big enough. |
| "Their pricing structure is confusing — I'll simplify" | They learned the hard way why it's structured that way. Copy it. |
| "I'll differentiate from day one" | Differentiate later. Copy exactly at the start. |

**Why copying works:** Someone who's been doing this for 15 years knows more than someone who's been doing it for 15 days. Assume competitors are smart, not stupid.

> "I don't assume that other competitors are idiots. I've been proven wrong too many times."

**The Flight Path Analogy:** If a plane starts deviating from its flight path at the beginning, it ends up hundreds of miles off course. But if it makes small tweaks along the way, it arrives close to or even better than the original destination. Copy exactly at the start. Innovate incrementally once you understand *why* things work.

### The E-Commerce Fulfillment Lesson

Chris started a fulfillment business and thought the competitors' pricing was needlessly complex — storage fees, pick-pack-ship fees, expiration tracking fees. He simplified to one flat fee.

Over months and years, he learned:
- Storage fees exist because customers go bankrupt and leave you holding inventory
- Pick-pack-ship fees exist because box complexity varies wildly
- Every "confusing" fee had a hard-learned reason behind it

> "At the end of our 2-year experiment, we looked just like all of our competitors. If we would have started there, we could have saved two years."

### 2. Old Problems vs. New Problems

| Type | Characteristics | Best Approach |
|------|----------------|--------------|
| **Old Problems** | Hiring, cash flow, legal, pricing, operations — thousands have come before you | Copy someone who solved it in the last decade. Hire expertise. Don't experiment. |
| **New Problems** | AI integration, new platforms, emerging markets — no established playbook | Experiment. Test. Iterate. This is where innovation belongs. |

**The critical mistake:** Founders waste years experimenting on old problems when they should spend all their experimentation energy on new problems and hire people to navigate old ones.

> "95% of things in business are old problems. The same solutions and same thinking is still relevant."

### 3. The Bias for Action Muscle

> "People should take their curious ideas very seriously. They need to shorten the amount of time between having the idea and doing something about it."

This is a trainable muscle. The more you shrink the gap between idea and action:
- The more ideas you'll have
- The more you'll test
- The more you'll learn tools
- The more surface area you'll create for success

It becomes a self-perpetuating snowball that compounds.

### 4. The Two-Week Rule

Chris's personal validation framework:

- Have an idea → Do research → Still thinking about it 2 weeks later?
- If yes: there's something here. Pursue it.
- If no (like the airport idea he forgot about): move on. Who cares.

**Signals it's worth pursuing:**
- You can't get it out of your head
- You see things in the world that add to the idea
- You're sending manic voice notes to friends about it
- It survives the "valley of despair" (initial excitement → doubt → if something pulls you back up, it's real)

### 5. The Three Types of Entrepreneurs

| Type | Description | Analogy |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Starter / Visionary** | Idea machine. Marketing idea guy. Subject line guy. Gets energy from launching. | The spark |
| **Maintainer / Operator** | Wakes up loving to tweak things. Process-oriented. Fixes small problems all day. | The engine |
| **Finisher / Deal Guy** | Super connector. Creative deals. Exits. Putting people together. Sees things to completion. | The closer |

**When all three exist in one person:** You get Mark Zuckerberg, Jensen Huang, Elon Musk — zero to trillion. Only ~3 people in history have done this.

**Know which one you are.** Chris is a starter. If he stays in a business too long, it falls apart. He needs to hand off at a specific point or have a partner who takes over.

### 6. Unfair Advantage Filter

> "I will only start businesses today where I have an unfair advantage."

Everyone has an unfair advantage in something — even a 13-year-old who knows Roblox better than anyone.

When choosing from a spreadsheet of ideas, prioritize ones where you have:
- Content/audience to grow it
- Friends in the space
- Unique knowledge or insight
- An existing network or reputation
- A "right to win"

## The Validation Playbook

### What Validation Actually Means

Validation = checking if the market gives a damn about what you're making, as fast as possible.

**The boulder analogy:**
- Most businesses: two steps forward, one step back (pushing boulder up hill). This is normal and fine.
- 5% of businesses: the boulder is chasing you downhill. You can't sleep because you're fulfilling orders. You're not distracted by the next shiny object. **That is product market fit.**

### The Facebook Marketplace Test

For any idea, the lowest-friction first step is Facebook Marketplace:

**Example: Women's creatine brand**

1. Use AI image generators to mock up what the product could look like (gummy version AND powder version)
2. Post both to Facebook Marketplace with a simple pitch: "Women's creatine that actually tastes good"
3. Track three metrics in a Google Sheet: views, clicks, messages
4. Within 2 hours, you'll know which form factor has more demand
5. After a day or two, boost both ads with $10 each — see if paid changes anything
6. Post 10 more variations: different photos, headlines, descriptions, price points
7. Track everything in the spreadsheet

**Concurrently:** Join Facebook groups (healthy moms, ultrarunner moms, moms who love rucking) and search for creatine, gummies, powders, price points, vendors.

**Then:** Find a co-packer, get samples based on the data, and go to a farmers market to get **in-person** feedback.

### In-Person Validation > Online Validation

> "What do they say versus how do they react? And is it the same?"

Online feedback from friends and family is unreliable. In-person validation is gold because you can observe:
1. **What they say** about your product
2. **Their body language** when they experience it
3. **Their facial reaction**

If all three overlap — either they're a psychopath or you have something special.

### Facebook Ads as a Foundational Skill

> "Facebook ads are like the infinite money glitch. It's a cheat code. We should know Facebook ads like we know how to write emails."

- You can become proficient in a couple of days if you focus on one thing to sell
- Learn by launching ads, not endlessly watching YouTube
- Very few people have this skill — that's why ad agencies charge so much

## Failure Patterns & Warnings

### The #1 Roadblock: Caring What People Think

> "The pain of your problem needs to be greater than how much you care about what people think about you."

Chris wouldn't post his side hustle #37 to Facebook because he thought his high school friends would roll their eyes. This self-imposed constraint meant he couldn't leverage his willing network.

### Mirage Opportunities

Ideas that seem obvious but never work for anyone:

> "If I see everybody trying to solve this particular problem and nobody's been successful yet, I stay away."

**Examples of mirage opportunities:**
- Group organizing apps (WhatsApp replacement for friend groups, run clubs, etc.) — huge obvious need, zero traction for anyone
- Password replacement technologies — everyone hates passwords, a dozen companies have tried, all failed
- "What's happening on campus" apps — every entrepreneurial college student tries this. Fails because you lose 33% of users to graduation annually and people decide what to do based on their friend group, not a notice board
- Apps/tools for podcasters — selling to a market where 95% quit within 6 months

**The test:** If a smart company like Amazon has a terrible UI, they probably know why it looks that way. Don't assume you're smarter than people who've been doing it for decades.

### The Premature Innovation Trap

Don't put your own spin on things at the start:
- If the competitor charges confusing prices → there's a reason
- If the competitor uses a specific channel → there's a reason
- If you change things on day one based on ego, you'll miss why the original model works

### The Equity Trap

> "50/50 partnerships are significantly harder than marriages, but we put 99% less thought into the structure."

**For 50/50 to work, ALL of these must be true forever:**
- Both are all-in for the entire lifespan of the business
- Same amount of money, effort, connections, value, history, background
- Both completely selfless about the other's contributions
- Both develop and grow at the same rate going forward

**The sweet spot:** Don't formalize equity too early (over avocado toast) or too late (2 years into revenue). Set a defined metric — like $10K in revenue — and then have the equity conversation based on real contributions.

> "Most equity goes to zero, period. I put a lot more emphasis on cash flow now than equity. Equity is overrated."

**For operators/partners:** Cut them in on quarterly profits rather than dangling equity. Equity is more likely to go to zero. Cash flow is real and motivating.

### Don't Burn the Boats Too Soon

There's a paradox here:

**The rational view:** Don't quit your $400K job to test something. There are enough tools to vet a side hustle on nights and weekends first. Wait until you see a clear path to scaling — ads converting, product-market fit proven, safety nets in place.

**The contradictory truth:** When boats have been burned for Chris (involuntarily), Plan A "just freaking thrives." Not because of removed safety nets, but because it puts a chip on your shoulder. Toxic motivation works.

**The resolution:** A side hustle making $50K/year on 20 hours/week might jump to $500K when you go to 40 hours. You double the hours but 10x the money because of the "burn the boats" effect. But wait until you can see the path before lighting the match.

## Key Insights & Principles

### "Test Everything Except Drugs"

Experimentation is the basis of everything. But apply it to the right category:
- **New problems** → experiment freely
- **Old problems** → copy what works, hire expertise

### Focus Is Overrated. Momentum Is Underrated.

> "The person who maintains momentum and gets energy from what they do is more likely to be a millionaire than the average person."

The greatest companies (Apple, Meta, Nvidia) were built with obsessive focus. But that produces billionaires, not millionaires. If your goal is freedom, presence, and a great life — momentum across multiple ventures may be the better path.

> "A lack of focus is a signal that there's something else out there. Pay attention to those signals of distraction."

### Follow the Profit Until You Can Follow the Passion

> "The statistical likelihood that what we love and what makes us money overlaps in the beginning is almost zero."

1. Build your passion around commerce itself — turning $1 into $2
2. Start anything. Get sweaty, ugly, active income
3. Over time, passive income becomes more realistic
4. Then start things in industries you're passionate about

### Entrepreneurship Is a Trade-Off, Not a Solution

> "There are no solutions, only trade-offs."

**What you trade for entrepreneurship:**
- Stability
- Predictability
- Lack of volatility
- Mental load of not knowing where your paycheck comes from

**What you get:**
- Optionality
- Potential for outsized returns
- Control over what you work on
- Dynamic, colorful life

**The uncomfortable truth:** You might be less free as an entrepreneur than in a 9-to-5. You answer to team members, investors, suppliers, clients. Many people love the *idea* of entrepreneurship but would hate the daily grind of it.

> "Many of the people that think they want to be an entrepreneur just like the idea of it."

### The Fishing Analogy

Entrepreneurship is a massive ocean with billions of fish. Most people cast a few times, catch nothing, and go home saying "entrepreneurship doesn't work."

But you haven't tested enough:
- Different depths
- Different baits
- Different smells
- McDonald's straws cut into fourths (literally worked for catching fish)

Stay out on the ocean. Try everything. Even if you don't catch anything, you learn. Eventually you have a boat full of sharks, grouper, and snapper — options, color, and an amazing life.

## Specific Business Ideas by Budget

### $500 Budget

#### 1. AI Implementation Agency
- 400M small businesses globally; 77% say AI would be transformational; only 5% are using it
- Spend $100 learning vibe coding tools (Replit, Lovable, Lindy)
- Build a simple website: "Chris's Automations"
- Spend $400 on local Facebook ads: "I will make your business more money or save your business money with AI"
- Get on discovery calls, learn their problems, build solutions with AI tools
- Charge $500–$5,000 upfront + 20% ongoing monthly maintenance
- **Example:** Build an AI voice agent for a gutter cleaning business so they don't miss after-hours calls. Charge $3,000 + $500-600/month. Copy-paste to every gutter cleaning business.

#### 2. Drop Servicing
- Create a website for a home service (garage repair, car repair, etc.)
- Run Facebook/Google ads to generate leads
- Subcontract all work to a local business that actually does the repairs
- You're the marketing arm; they're the fulfillment arm
- **Example:** Bob's oil change costs $40. Your clean website charges $80. You send Bob $40, keep $40. Customer pays for better UI and checkout experience.

#### 3. Directory Websites
- Scrape every dog park / ice supplier / niche service in a city
- Build a directory website with Replit or Lovable
- No paid ads — wait for SEO to drive organic traffic
- Monetize with Google display ads or charge businesses for priority placement
- Numbers game: build dozens. Some make $0, some make thousands.
- Very passive once built.

### $1,000 Budget

#### 4. Wedding Rental Business
- Build a wedding arch from lumber for $200-300
- Rent it out at $1,000 per wedding
- Find clients through wedding planners (scrape from The Knot, Zola, Wedding Wire)
- One planner contact = one wedding per week
- Expand to: photo walls, charcuterie carts, custom pizza setups
- Also works for funerals (population increasing = demand increasing)

#### 5. Local Email Newsletter
- Spend $1,000 on Facebook ads targeting a local area (e.g., Boise, Idaho)
- Acquire ~1,000 subscribers at ~$1 each
- Free email software trial (Beehiiv, ConvertKit)
- Send one weekly email: what's happening locally, discounts, news
- Write it yourself in natural language — NOT ChatGPT (needs to feel like a local wrote it)
- Revenue: ~$0.50/subscriber/month from ads = $500/month
- Break even in 2 months, then scale to other cities
- 10% of subscribers are business owners = 100 potential sponsors in your list

### $5,000 Budget

#### 6. Small RV Park Acquisition
- Call every small RV park (3-10 pads) within a three-hour drive
- For every 100 parks contacted, ~2-3 are good deals
- Use seller financing (seller holds the note; you make monthly payments from profits)
- $2,000 for due diligence (inspections, title policy)
- Remainder for operating capital (power bills, tree trimming, cleanup)
- Underperforming parks have no website, undermarket rents, occupancy issues
- Fix with: free Facebook Marketplace ads, free vibe-coded website, rent increases
- Buy at 10% cap rate ($200K park making $20K/year profit) → improve to 8% cap rate → park now worth $360K+
- 3-10x more profitable than single-family rental homes with more distributed risk

## The Vending Machine Business

- Buy a used machine on Facebook Marketplace for $300-400
- Stock with Costco/Sam's Club snacks for ~$100
- Credit card reader can cost as much as the machine itself
- Get one location (hardest part) → then leverage that track record for more
- **Best locations:** Apartment complex lobbies (high volume), not auto repair shops
- **What to stock:** People want Doritos and Coke, not healthy options (tested and failed in health-conscious San Diego)
- **Scale economics:** 100 machines → 20 make $3,000/month each, 80 make $400/month each
- Can become passive with an employee to restock

## Quick Reference: Principles That Cut Across Everything

1. **Copy what works** — start where your competitor is today, innovate later
2. **Test everything** — Facebook Marketplace is the lowest-friction testing ground on earth
3. **Shorten the idea-to-action gap** — this muscle compounds over time
4. **Old problems: copy. New problems: experiment.** — don't waste years reinventing solved problems
5. **Follow the profit, then the passion** — commerce itself can be your passion
6. **Focus is overrated, momentum is underrated** — unless you want to be a billionaire
7. **Beware mirage opportunities** — if nobody's made it work despite obvious demand, something fundamental is off
8. **In-person validation beats online feedback** — watch faces, not read comments
9. **Facebook ads are a foundational life skill** — the infinite money glitch
10. **Entrepreneurship is a trade-off, not a solution** — know what you're trading before you trade it
