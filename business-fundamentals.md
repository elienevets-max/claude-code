# The Five Parts of Every Business

> "Every business from the smallest startup to the largest corporation in the world has five fundamental parts." — Josh Kaufman, The Personal MBA

This is the foundation of every business plan that has or ever will exist. Each part is **universal, essential, and fundamental**.

---

## 1. Value Creation

**Find important unmet needs and meet them.**

- The first question: *What problem are you solving?*
- Understand what people actually want, need, and are looking for
- Observe behavior — people's actions reveal more than their words
- Make trade-offs between competing priorities (quality, price, durability, aesthetics)
- Most successful businesses pick 1-2 dimensions and turn that dial to 9, 10, 11

### The Observation Principle

The most valuable insights come from watching how people actually behave, not from asking what they want in a boardroom.

**Case Study — Liquid Laundry Detergent:**
A Procter & Gamble researcher watched a woman do laundry. She waited 15-20 seconds for the tub to fill before adding powdered detergent, then swished it with her hand to dissolve it. The chemists said this was scientifically unnecessary — the powder dissolved fine. But the consumer researchers recognized an *unmet psychological need* for reassurance. The result: liquid laundry detergent, now a multi-billion dollar product category.

### How to Find Business Ideas

1. Start with problems you've personally experienced
2. Go out into the world with your eyes open — look for annoyances, frictions, suboptimal situations
3. Become a fly on the wall — observe people using products and services
4. Notice the subtle things people might not even pick up on themselves
5. Humanity is not running out of problems to solve

### Critical Validation

> "Show me a credit card that has been swiped to solve this problem and I will concede that the problem is real." — Des Traynor, Intercom

- Friends and family saying "I'd buy one" means nothing
- The real signal: **Are people willing to pay money to solve this problem?**
- Ask for pre-orders before building at scale
- Use mechanisms like Kickstarter or letters of intent to test buying signals
- Start with small batches to mitigate risk
- Collect feedback from early customers before scaling

### The Iron Law of the Market

> "Markets that don't exist don't care how smart you are." — Marc Andreessen

- Competition is a **good sign** — it validates that a market exists
- If many people are selling candles, it means many people are willing to buy candles
- Be cautious about "nobody has ever seen anything like this" — that could be a warning sign
- You need to educate and convince people AND have no idea if they'll actually purchase
- **Case Study — Segway:** Extreme hype, predicted to change city planning. But people didn't want a $10,000 alternative to walking or riding a bike.

### How to Treat Competitors

1. **Learn from them** — buy their products, become their customer
2. **Deconstruct everything** — packaging, labeling, price points, distribution, advertising
3. Combine competitor research with your own customer research
4. Find your unique take, taste, and twist
5. All of this market research is essentially free (aside from time and small budget)

## 2. Marketing

**Attract the attention of people who might be interested in the value you're creating.**

If no one knows you exist, no one can buy your thing.

### The Core Challenge

Most people moving through their lives are busy, stressed, and overwhelmed. They already have too much information bombarding them. Your job is to cut through that noise.

**The primary skill of marketing:** Highlight the thing that's most important, most interesting, most valuable — upfront, quickly, in a way that cuts through.

### Five Core Human Drives

Every purchase hooks into one or more of these drives. The more drives your offer connects to, the more attractive it becomes.

| Drive | Description | Examples |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Acquire** | Get things, enhance status, collect | Products, luxury goods, career advancement |
| **Bond** | Connect, belong, form relationships | Community, group identity, social experiences |
| **Learn** | Understand, discover, satisfy curiosity | Education, tools, information products |
| **Defend** | Protect, secure, feel safe | Insurance, security systems, privacy features |
| **Feel** | Experience emotions, have sensory experiences | Entertainment, experiences, aesthetics |

**Why social media is so powerful:** It hooks into at least 4 out of 5 drives, potentially all five.

### Case Study — Apple

| Drive | How Apple Hooks Into It |
|-------|------------------------|
| Acquire | Status product — always has been |
| Bond | Advertising about connecting people |
| Learn | Marketing emphasizes learning capabilities (iPad) |
| Defend | Heavy focus on encryption, security, and privacy |
| Feel | Spatial video, emotional product experiences |

**Early iPod ads** (white silhouette dancing): Pure emotion. "A thousand songs in your pocket" — not "1GB hard drive." They made a deliberate decision to skip the technical specs because *people didn't care*.

### Counter-Signaling Strategy

Be the **antithesis** of the incumbents in your market.

- Make it clear you are an "or" — not an "and" — in your customer's decision framework
- The customer should think: "I can be a [incumbent brand] person OR a [your brand] person — I can't be both"
- This takes guts but creates distinctive positioning with a fraction of the marketing budget

**Examples:**
- **Liquid Death:** Just water in a different can. Built a billion-dollar business through pure counter-signaling against traditional water brands
- **BrewDog:** Took competitor beers to a field and blew them up with dynamite. "We are not commoditized beer."
- **Apple's 1984 ad:** Pure counter-signaling against faceless, beige computer box corporations

> "We need to piss off the 80% to get to the 20%." — Jane Wurwand, Dermalogica

### Key Marketing Principles

1. **You don't need everyone** — you need the tiny fraction who care more than everyone else
2. **Polarization is good** — it means your offering is distinctive enough that people who care *really* care
3. **Controversy is energy** — it makes people make a decision (use responsibly)
4. **Focus on hyper-responders** — people who care a lot, care often, and are really into it
5. **Don't be vanilla wallpaper** — most struggling businesses are struggling because they're not distinctive

## 3. Sales

**Convince people to buy and get them set up as customers.**

Marketing attracts attention. Sales converts attention into revenue. Sales is the only part where money comes **into** the business.

### Features vs Benefits

This is the most popular and most important sales framework.

| | Definition | Example (Candles) | Example (iPod) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | What the product is/has | Made from 100% soy wax | 1GB hard drive |
| **Benefit** | What the customer gets/feels | Environmentally conscious, clean burning | A thousand songs in your pocket |

**The rule:** Lead with benefits. Use features only as *reasons to believe* the benefit will be delivered.

### Why Benefits Win

Before making a purchase, people **mentally simulate** what their life will look like with the product.

- Buying a car: you imagine pulling it into your driveway, what the neighbors will think
- This is why stories work in marketing and sales — they engage the brain's simulation and prediction processes
- **Car sales rule #1:** Get the potential customer in the driver's seat as quickly as possible

> Anything you can do to engage the customer's simulation and prediction processes, the better.

### The Happy Customer Principle

A sale is not just to get a customer — it's to get a **happy, satisfied customer**.

- The first sale is the most expensive
- Repeat customers are the best customers: they know you exist, know your product is great, and don't require re-acquisition
- **Lifetime Value (LTV):** The sum total of all sales from one customer over their entire relationship with your business
- Customer service is not an annoyance — it's a profit center
- Happy customers are your primary source of word-of-mouth marketing

### Customer Reactivation

One of the most effective and cheapest marketing/sales campaigns:

> "Hey, you bought candles from us last year. Would you like to buy candles from us this year?"

- You already know they exist
- You already know they've purchased
- The cost is minimal compared to acquiring new customers

## 4. Value Delivery

**Deliver the value you promised to your paying customers.**

- This is where you fulfill the promise made during marketing and sales
- The quality of delivery directly impacts repeat business and word-of-mouth
- Post-sale support is critical — not an afterthought
- A venture that markets and sells something that does not deliver value is a scam

## 5. Finance

**Track money in, money out, and ensure sustainability.**

### Core Financial Concepts

All of business finance comes down to **common sense and simple arithmetic**: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division.

**The essential calculation:**
```
Revenue (what you sell it for)
- Cost of goods (what it costs to make)
- Cost of marketing (what it costs to attract attention)
- Cost of delivery (what it costs to fulfill)
= Profit (what you keep)
```

### Key Numbers to Track (Early Stage)

1. **Monthly fixed overhead** — everything required to keep the lights on (rent, tools, subscriptions, everything)
2. **Monthly sales** — how much is coming in from product/service sales
3. **Cost to deliver** — what it costs to fulfill each sale
4. **Net profit** — how much you get to keep after everything

### The Sustainability Question

> "Is it enough to make all of the time, energy, and effort worthwhile?"

- Based on your life and lifestyle, what's the number that makes the venture long-term sustainable?
- If you're working 80 hours/week and have $5 at the end of the month — technically profitable, but not sustainable
- Focus on getting to your sustainability number first
- From there, you have many different options

### Financial Decision-Making

- How much do you have at your disposal?
- What are the critical things that must be met?
- How do you allocate between product, marketing, sales, and delivery?
- Every allocation is a trade-off

## Putting It All Together

### Understanding business is a superpower because:

1. You can take any complex organization and break it down into simple, understandable parts
2. You can ask the right questions about any business
3. You understand what matters and what doesn't
4. You can think like an owner/operator — which is what executives look for in hiring and promoting
5. It's the foundation of every business plan that has or ever will exist

### The Playing Business Trap

Don't confuse **playing business** with **doing business**:

| Playing Business | Doing Business |
|------------------|----------------|
| Picking a logo | Creating value |
| Designing business cards | Marketing to real customers |
| Setting up a fancy office | Making sales |
| Spending 2-3 years building before launch | Delivering value |
| Raising money before validation | Managing finances |

> Skip the signaling. Get to the core of what will make the business succeed or fail. Figure that out as quickly as possible.

### Gall's Law

> "Any complex system that works evolved from a simpler system that worked."

- Start simple — it's actually the sophisticated thing to do
- Every additional bit of complexity needs to earn its way
- Complexity isn't value — more features don't automatically mean more value
- **Case Study — Whoop:** Deliberately didn't add a clock display. Adding the time would change the frame from "health tracker" to "watch" — creating entirely new expectations.

### The Experimentation Mindset

**Increasing your rate of experimentation increases your rate of success.**

The Explore/Exploit Trade-off (from computer science):
1. **Explore phase:** Try many different things, collect information
2. **Exploit phase:** Use knowledge to do the best-performing things more often
3. **Key insight:** The exploration phase never fully stops — it just gets smaller over time

**Critical:** Experimentation without feedback is wasted time. The power is in collecting and acting on information.

## Quick Reference: Business Diagnostic Questions

For any business, ask:

1. **Value Creation:** How is this business creating value? What problem does it solve?
2. **Marketing:** How does it attract attention from people who want/need what it provides?
3. **Sales:** How does it convince people to pay? What's the buying process?
4. **Value Delivery:** How does it deliver the promised value to paying customers?
5. **Finance:** Is it bringing in more money than it spends? Is it enough to be sustainable and worthwhile?

If any of these questions can't be answered satisfactorily, there's a problem — and now you know exactly where to look.
