> "We don't rise to good decisions — we fall into bad ones unless we actively prevent them." — Rolf Dobelli (paraphrased)

## The Core Insight

Human thinking is systematically flawed in predictable, repeatable ways, and intelligence does not protect you — often it makes the flaws worse. Most bad decisions are not caused by lack of information, but by cognitive biases operating invisibly. Clear thinking is therefore not about being clever; it's about removing sources of error. The rational person builds filters, rules, and constraints to catch biases before they act. Avoid stupidity first; brilliance is optional.

---

## 5 Most Important Takeaways

1. **Cognitive errors are systematic and predictable.** They follow patterns. Learn the patterns, catch them before they cost you.
2. **Survivorship bias contaminates every success story.** You only see the winners. The failures who used the same strategy are invisible. Before learning from success, find the failures.
3. **Action bias is as dangerous as inaction.** Doing something feels productive even when waiting is optimal. Ask: "Am I acting because it's right, or because doing nothing feels wrong?"
4. **More information doesn't improve decisions past a point.** Beyond the critical 20%, additional research increases confidence without increasing accuracy. Know when to stop and decide.
5. **Most perceived patterns are random noise.** Short streaks, small clusters, and vivid anecdotes are not data. Require statistical significance before changing strategy.

## Rapid Bias Detection System

### SITUATION 1: Evaluating Whether Something Worked

**Biases that attack here:**

| Bias | What It Does | Diagnostic Question |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Survivorship Bias** | You're only seeing the winners — failures are invisible | "How many tried this and failed? Can I find the failures?" |
| **Outcome Bias** | Judging by result, not by decision quality at the time | "Would I evaluate this differently if the outcome were reversed?" |
| **Halo Effect** | One impressive quality coloring your entire assessment | "Is one quality (charisma, revenue, fame) making me overlook weaknesses?" |
| **Story Bias** | A clean narrative replacing complex, multicausal reality | "Is the explanation suspiciously clean and linear? What complexity has been stripped away?" |
| **Swimmer's Body Illusion** | Confusing selection effects with results | "Did the process CAUSE the success, or did it SELECT for people likely to succeed anyway?" |
| **Clustering Illusion** | Reading meaning into random streaks | "Is this a real trend or normal variance in a small sample?" |

### SITUATION 2: About to Commit Resources (Time, Money, Energy)

**Biases that attack here:**

| Bias | What It Does | Diagnostic Question |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Action Bias** | Doing something feels better than waiting, even when waiting is optimal | "Am I acting because analysis supports it, or because inaction feels irresponsible?" |
| **Sunk Cost Fallacy** | Continuing because of past investment, not future value | "If I hadn't already invested, would I start this today?" |
| **Effort Justification** | Valuing something more because you worked hard on it | "Am I defending this because it's good, or because I put effort into it?" |
| **Planning Fallacy** | Underestimating time, cost, and risk | "What did similar projects actually cost and take? (Historical data, not my optimistic plan)" |
| **Overconfidence** | Overestimating your knowledge, ability, or control | "How confident am I, and is that confidence calibrated or am I in my own hype?" |
| **Zero-Risk Bias** | Preferring complete elimination of small risk over greater reduction of larger one | "Am I trying to eliminate a small risk when I should be reducing a bigger one?" |
| **Paradox of Choice** | Too many options causing paralysis or regret | "Do I have clear criteria, or am I drowning in options?" |

### SITUATION 3: In a Group Setting

**Biases that attack here:**

| Bias | What It Does | Diagnostic Question |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Groupthink** | Desire for harmony suppressing dissent and narrowing options | "Would I reach this conclusion alone with the same evidence?" |
| **Social Proof** | Following the group instead of evaluating independently | "Am I agreeing because the evidence compels me or because everyone else agrees?" |
| **Authority Bias** | Deferring to status/title instead of evidence | "Is the most senior person driving the conclusion? Would I disagree if they held a different view?" |
| **Not-Invented-Here** | Dismissing external ideas in favor of internal ones | "Have I dismissed any ideas specifically because they came from outside the team?" |
| **Social Comparison** | Evaluating relative to peers instead of on absolute merit | "Am I optimizing for absolute performance or for looking better than a peer?" |

### SITUATION 4: Processing New Information

**Biases that attack here:**

| Bias | What It Does | Diagnostic Question |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Confirmation Bias** | Seeking/favoring information that supports existing beliefs | "Am I seeking truth or seeking confirmation?" |
| **Information Bias** | Gathering more data to feel productive when you already have enough to decide | "Would more information actually change my decision? If not, stop and decide." |
| **Story Bias / Narrative Fallacy** | Converting complex reality into simple linear stories | "Is this explanation a satisfying story or an accurate model? What's been left out?" |
| **Conjunction Fallacy** | Finding specific, detailed scenarios more plausible than general ones | "Does a detailed scenario feel more probable than it should? (More detail = less probable)" |
| **Domain Dependence** | Failing to transfer rational thinking from one domain to another | "Am I applying the same rigor here that I use in my strongest domain?" |
| **Availability Heuristic** | Overweighting vivid, recent, or easily recalled information | "Am I overweighting this because it's vivid or recent, not because it's important?" |
| **Recency Bias** | Overweighting the latest data point relative to the full dataset | "Am I giving the latest data point more weight than the full history warrants?" |

### SITUATION 5: Assessing Risk

**Biases that attack here:**

| Bias | What It Does | Diagnostic Question |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Neglect of Probability** | Responding to magnitude of outcomes, ignoring their likelihood | "Am I responding to probability × magnitude, or to how the risk FEELS?" |
| **Zero-Risk Bias** | Wanting to eliminate small risks entirely instead of reducing larger ones | "Am I trying to eliminate a small risk when larger risks remain unaddressed?" |
| **Black Swan Blindness** | Assuming the future will resemble the past | "What assumptions about the future am I making? What if one breaks?" |
| **Overconfidence** | Underestimating ranges and overestimating prediction accuracy | "How wide should my confidence interval really be? (Wider than I think)" |
| **Gambler's Fallacy** | Believing independent events are connected (due for a win after losses) | "Are these events independent? Does the streak actually change the underlying probability?" |
| **Hot Hand Fallacy** | Believing a streak reflects changed underlying probability | "Is this a genuine skill signal, or just variance in a small sample?" |

### SITUATION 6: Comparing Options

**Biases that attack here:**

| Bias | What It Does | Diagnostic Question |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Contrast Effect** | Judging relative to a nearby comparison, not on absolute merit | "Am I judging this on absolute merit or relative to something that makes it look better?" |
| **Anchoring** | First number encountered skewing all subsequent estimates | "Was there a number that set an anchor? What would I estimate independently?" |
| **Framing Effect** | Identical information presented differently changing the decision | "Would I decide differently if this same information were framed the other way?" |
| **Decoy Effect** | An irrelevant option making another option look better | "Is one of these options here just to make another look good?" |
| **Social Comparison** | Evaluating against others' outcomes instead of own criteria | "Am I comparing to someone else's results instead of my own criteria?" |
| **Endowment Effect** | Overvaluing what you already have simply because you have it | "Am I overvaluing what I currently have just because it's mine?" |

## Key Concepts

### Survivorship Bias

**What it is:** Systematic overestimation of success probability because failures are invisible. You see the winners; the losers who used the same approach disappeared from the dataset.

**When to apply:** Evaluating any success story, case study, business advice, or strategy recommendation.

**How to apply:** Ask: "How many tried this and failed? Can I find the failures?" If you can't find counterexamples, the success story tells you almost nothing about the strategy's actual effectiveness.

| With Survivorship Bias | Without It |
|----------------------|-----------|
| "This strategy clearly works — look at the successes" | "What's the failure rate for this strategy?" |
| "Follow the winners" | "Where are the losers who did the same thing?" |
| "The evidence is overwhelming" | "The evidence is filtered — I'm missing the counterexamples" |

### The Swimmer's Body Illusion

**What it is:** Confusing selection effects with causal results. The process didn't create the outcome — it selected for people who were already predisposed to that outcome.

**When to apply:** Evaluating training programs, coaching, schools, tools, or any system that claims to produce results.

**How to apply:** Ask: "Does this system CREATE success, or does it ATTRACT people who were likely to succeed anyway?" Harvard doesn't make people successful — it selects people who were going to be successful.

| Selection Effect | Causal Effect |
|-----------------|--------------|
| System attracts winners | System creates winners |
| Success preceded enrollment | Success followed enrollment |
| Correlation with selection criteria | Correlation with training content |
| Test: Remove the system — would these people still succeed? | Test: Apply the system to random people — does it work? |

### Action Bias

**What it is:** The compulsion to do something in ambiguous situations, even when doing nothing is optimal.

**When to apply:** Any time you feel pressure to act and can't articulate why acting is better than waiting.

**How to apply:** Ask: "Am I acting because it's the right move, or because inaction feels irresponsible?" If you can't articulate a specific reason why acting NOW is better than acting LATER with more information, the action bias is driving you.

| Action Bias | Deliberate Action |
|------------|------------------|
| "I need to DO something" | "Analysis says this specific action is optimal" |
| Feels urgent without clear reason | Urgency has identifiable cause |
| Acting relieves anxiety | Acting follows evidence |
| Can't articulate why now vs. later | Can explain time-sensitivity |

### Domain Dependence

**What it is:** Failure to transfer rational thinking tools from one domain to another. Being brilliant at poker analysis but impulsive in business decisions.

**When to apply:** When entering any new domain or making decisions outside your primary field.

**How to apply:** Ask: "Am I applying the same standards of evidence and reasoning I use in my strongest domain?" Deliberately port your best thinking tools into new contexts.

| Domain Dependent | Domain Independent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Rigorous at poker, impulsive in business | Same reasoning standards everywhere |
| "This is different" | "The same principles apply" |
| Expertise creates false security in adjacent areas | Expertise creates transferable thinking tools |

### Information Bias

**What it is:** Continuing to gather information past the point where it improves decision quality, because research feels productive and delays the anxiety of deciding.

**When to apply:** When you've been "researching" for a while and still haven't decided.

**How to apply:** Ask: "If I got this additional information, would it actually change my decision?" If no, stop researching and decide. The 80/20 rule applies: you usually have enough information to make a quality decision much earlier than it feels like you do.

| Productive Research | Information Bias |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Each new data point could change the decision | Already know enough to decide |
| Specific questions with known sources | Browsing for "more" without specifics |
| Time-boxed | Open-ended |
| Reduces uncertainty | Increases confidence without reducing uncertainty |

### Clustering Illusion

**What it is:** Perceiving meaningful patterns in random data. Short streaks, small clusters, and coincidences feel significant but are usually noise.

**When to apply:** Evaluating short-term results in any variance-heavy domain — poker, investing, marketing campaigns, sales.

**How to apply:** Ask: "Would this pattern be surprising if the underlying process were completely random?" Calculate whether the sample size is large enough for statistical significance. Usually it isn't.

| Signal | Noise |
|--------|-------|
| Persists across large samples | Disappears with more data |
| Explainable mechanism | "It just feels meaningful" |
| Statistically significant | Normal variance |
| Replicable | One-time observation |

### Effort Justification

**What it is:** Valuing something more because you put effort into it, independent of actual quality.

**When to apply:** When you or someone else is defending work by citing the effort invested rather than the quality of the output.

**How to apply:** Evaluate outputs by quality and results, not by hours invested. When you hear "but we spent months on this," that's effort justification, not evidence of value.

### Social Comparison Bias

**What it is:** Evaluating yourself relative to peers rather than on absolute terms, leading to irrational competitive behavior.

**When to apply:** When you notice your satisfaction or strategy being influenced by someone else's results.

**How to apply:** Set absolute benchmarks in advance. Ask: "Does their outcome actually change anything about my situation?" Define "enough" before you start comparing.

## The Master Pre-Flight Checklist

Run this before any decision where the stakes are meaningful.

### Evidence Quality
- [ ] Am I only seeing survivors? Where are the failures? *(Survivorship)*
- [ ] Am I judging by outcome or decision quality? *(Outcome Bias)*
- [ ] Is this a real pattern or normal randomness? *(Clustering Illusion)*
- [ ] Is the explanation a clean story or an accurate model? *(Story Bias)*
- [ ] Did the system create the success, or select for it? *(Swimmer's Body)*

### My Reasoning
- [ ] Am I seeking confirmation or genuinely testing? *(Confirmation Bias)*
- [ ] Do I need more information, or am I using research to avoid deciding? *(Information Bias)*
- [ ] Am I applying the same reasoning standards I use in my strongest domain? *(Domain Dependence)*
- [ ] Am I overweighting something because it's vivid or recent? *(Availability / Recency)*
- [ ] Is a detailed scenario feeling more probable than it should? *(Conjunction Fallacy)*

### My Motivations
- [ ] Am I acting because it's right, or because doing nothing feels wrong? *(Action Bias)*
- [ ] Am I continuing because it makes sense, or because I've already invested? *(Sunk Cost)*
- [ ] Am I defending this because it's good, or because I worked hard on it? *(Effort Justification)*
- [ ] Would I start this today if I weren't already in it? *(Status Quo Bias)*
- [ ] Am I being honest about my confidence level? *(Overconfidence)*

### Social Influences
- [ ] Would I reach this conclusion alone? *(Social Proof / Groupthink)*
- [ ] Am I deferring to status instead of evidence? *(Authority Bias)*
- [ ] Am I comparing to peers instead of my own criteria? *(Social Comparison)*
- [ ] Have I dismissed ideas because of their source? *(Not-Invented-Here)*

### Risk Assessment
- [ ] Am I responding to probability × magnitude, or to fear? *(Neglect of Probability)*
- [ ] What assumptions about the future am I making? What if one breaks? *(Black Swan Blindness)*
- [ ] Does this work even if 1-2 things go wrong? *(Margin of Safety)*
- [ ] Am I treating independent events as connected? *(Gambler's Fallacy)*

## Decision Frameworks

### Bias-First Analysis

**Use when:** Any major decision before committing.

**Process:**
1. Define the decision clearly
2. Identify which biases are most likely active (use the Situation guides above)
3. Apply the relevant counter-rule or diagnostic question
4. Decide only after the dominant bias is neutralized

**Key rule:** Assume bias is present. The question is WHICH bias, not WHETHER bias exists.

### The Luck Audit

**Use when:** Reviewing past decisions — wins or losses.

**Process:**
1. Separate outcome from decision quality
2. Ask: "Would I repeat this decision with the same information?"
3. If yes regardless of outcome → good decision (even if bad outcome)
4. If no regardless of outcome → bad decision (even if good outcome)

**Prevents:** False learning from lucky wins and false abandonment of sound strategies after unlucky losses.

### Counter-Anchoring

**Use when:** Negotiations, pricing, forecasts — any situation where a number has been introduced.

**Process:**
1. Generate your own estimate BEFORE seeing others'
2. Write it down privately
3. Compare to the anchor and note the pull
4. Decide based on your independent estimate, not the anchor

### The Kill-the-Idea Drill

**Use when:** You feel excited or attached to a plan.

**Process:**
1. Actively try to disprove your preferred option
2. Ask: "What evidence would force me to abandon this?"
3. If you can't generate credible counterarguments, you're not thinking — you're defending

## Tactical Playbooks

### Quick Bias Scan (2 Minutes)

For everyday decisions that don't warrant the full checklist:

1. **Name the situation:** Am I evaluating success? Committing resources? In a group? Processing info? Assessing risk? Comparing options?
2. **Check the top bias for that situation:** Survivorship? Sunk cost? Social proof? Confirmation? Neglect of probability? Anchoring?
3. **Ask the one diagnostic question** from the relevant situation table
4. **Decide**

### Post-Decision Review (5 Minutes)

After any significant decision:

1. What was the decision quality independent of outcome?
2. Which biases were most likely active?
3. Did any bias actually influence the decision?
4. What structural safeguard would prevent this next time?

### The "Would I Start Today?" Test

For any ongoing commitment that feels questionable:

1. Ignore all past investment (time, money, effort, emotion)
2. Ask: "If I were not already doing this, would I start today with what I know now?"
3. If no → the sunk cost fallacy is keeping you in
4. Exit or restructure

## Principles & Rules of Thumb

- **"Cognitive errors are systematic, not random."** They follow predictable patterns. Learn the patterns, catch them.
- **"Track record beats narrative."** Documented results against measurable outcomes are the only evidence that matters. Stories are not evidence.
- **"Find the failures before trusting the success story."** Survivorship bias contaminates every piece of advice, case study, and strategy recommendation.
- **"More information past the critical 20% increases confidence, not accuracy."** Know when to stop researching and decide.
- **"If you can't name the bias, you can't fight it."** The value of a bias catalog is pattern recognition speed. Name it to tame it.
- **"Random noise looks like a signal in small samples."** Require statistical significance before attributing meaning to patterns.
- **"Action is not inherently better than inaction."** Sometimes the most productive thing you can do is wait deliberately.
- **"Effort is not evidence of quality."** Hours invested don't validate an output. Results do.
- **"Transfer your best thinking tools to every domain."** If you're rigorous in one area and sloppy in another, the sloppiness is a choice.
- **"Every comparison is a frame."** What you compare against determines how you evaluate. Choose your reference points deliberately.
- **"Confidence tracks familiarity, not truth."** Never trust confidence as evidence of correctness.
- **"Statistics beat stories."** Vivid anecdotes distort probability assessment. Base rates are your friend.
- **"Urgency amplifies bias."** Avoid major decisions under time pressure when possible.
- **"Assume bias is present."** The question is which bias, not whether bias exists.

## Key Distinctions

| X | Y | Why It Matters | Test |
|---|---|---------------|------|
| **Skill** | **Luck** | Misattribution leads to overconfidence or false despair | Is the result repeatable over large samples? |
| **Information** | **Signal** | More data ≠ better decisions | Does this data point meaningfully change the probability? |
| **Loss** | **Missed gain** | Loss aversion distorts risk appetite | Would you take this bet if framed neutrally? |
| **Correlation** | **Causation** | Humans are pattern-hungry | Can you explain the mechanism? |
| **Pattern** | **Noise** | Small samples produce compelling but meaningless clusters | Would this be surprising if the process were random? |
| **Awareness** | **Protection** | Knowing about biases ≠ being protected from them | Do you have structural safeguards, not just knowledge? |
| **Confidence** | **Accuracy** | Feeling sure ≠ being right | What's your calibration track record? |
| **Selection effect** | **Causal effect** | The system attracts winners vs. creates winners | Would this work on random participants? |

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | What Happens | Instead |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Reading about biases and assuming you're protected** | Knowing names doesn't prevent them | Use structural safeguards: checklists, pre-committed criteria, external review |
| **Applying bias detection unevenly** | Spotting biases in others while blind to your own | Apply the same diagnostic questions to yourself |
| **Treating every decision as high-stakes** | Analysis paralysis on trivial choices | Reserve deep bias-checking for significant, irreversible decisions |
| **Using bias accusations as debate weapons** | "That's just confirmation bias" dismisses without engaging | Name the bias AND address the substance |
| **Paralysis from too much meta-cognition** | Spending so long checking for biases that you never decide | Time-box the bias check: 5 minutes for pre-flight, then decide |
| **Treating awareness as a structural safeguard** | "I'll remember to check" without a system | Build checklists, default rules, and external accountability |
| **Confusing explanation with prediction** | A good story about the past ≠ ability to predict the future | Demand forward-looking testable predictions, not backward-looking narratives |
| **More thinking inside a biased frame** | Reasoning harder doesn't fix a broken frame | Change the frame or pause the decision entirely |

## Connections to Other Skills

### → Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman)
Kahneman is the deep science; Dobelli is the flashcard deck. Use Kahneman to understand WHY biases work (System 1/System 2, prospect theory, dual-process architecture). Use Dobelli to quickly NAME which bias is operating. **Kahneman is the textbook. Dobelli is the field guide.**

### → Rationality: From AI to Zombies (Yudkowsky)
Yudkowsky provides the epistemological framework for what good reasoning looks like. Dobelli provides the catalog of specific ways reasoning fails. Yudkowsky goes deeper on WHY knowing about biases isn't enough (structural debiasing). Dobelli is useful precisely because sometimes you just need the quick name and counter-rule.

### → The Scout Mindset (Galef)
Galef answers WHY you resist updating — which emotional need the bias is serving (comfort, self-esteem, morale, belonging, persuasion). Dobelli tells you WHICH bias is operating. **Use Dobelli to detect, Galef to understand the resistance, and Galef's thought experiments to overcome it.**

### → Poor Charlie's Almanack (Munger)
Munger's unique addition is lollapalooza — biases don't operate independently, they converge multiplicatively. After using Dobelli to identify individual biases, use Munger's framework to check if multiple biases are stacking in the same direction. Also: Munger adds the incentive layer that Dobelli mostly omits.

### → Superforecasting (Tetlock)
Tetlock's method (base rates, decomposition, updating, scoring) is the systematic antidote to the biases Dobelli catalogs. **Use Dobelli to detect the error, Tetlock to correct it.**

### → Poker Strategy
This IS the poker player's session review checklist:
- **Outcome bias** → judging calls by results instead of decision quality
- **Clustering illusion** → finding meaning in short-term streaks
- **Action bias** → playing too many hands because folding feels passive
- **Sunk cost** → calling because chips are already invested
- **Information bias** → overthinking simple spots
- **Survivorship bias** → copying visible winners without seeing the losers
- **Social comparison** → measuring against others instead of your own strategy
- **Domain dependence** → rigorous at the table, impulsive in business

### → Business Strategy
Before applying any framework from any skill, run the relevant situation diagnostic from this skill. About to commit resources? Check Situation 2. Evaluating a case study? Check Situation 1. In a strategy meeting? Check Situation 3. Processing market data? Check Situation 4.
