> "A reliable way to make people believe in falsehoods is frequent repetition, because familiarity is not easily distinguished from truth." — Daniel Kahneman

## The Core Insight

Your brain runs two systems simultaneously. **System 1** is fast, automatic, and intuitive — it handles 98% of your thinking. **System 2** is slow, deliberate, and effortful — it handles complex calculations and careful reasoning. Most of your worst decisions happen when System 1 answers a hard question by substituting an easier one, and you don't notice.

---

## Two Systems of Thinking

### System 1 — Fast Thinking

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Speed** | Instant, automatic, effortless |
| **When it activates** | Always running — you can't turn it off |
| **Strengths** | Pattern recognition, snap judgments, emotional reads, expert intuition |
| **Weaknesses** | Jumps to conclusions, sees causality where there is none, systematically biased |
| **Examples** | Reading facial expressions, driving on an empty road, "gut feelings," recognizing a bluff |

System 1 generates **impressions, feelings, and inclinations**. When endorsed by System 2, these become beliefs, attitudes, and intentions.

### System 2 — Slow Thinking

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Speed** | Slow, serial, effortful |
| **When it activates** | Only when System 1 can't handle the problem (or when you deliberately engage it) |
| **Strengths** | Logic, math, complex reasoning, self-control, checking System 1's work |
| **Weaknesses** | Lazy — defaults to endorsing System 1; limited capacity; fatigues quickly |
| **Examples** | Calculating pot odds, comparing investment options, filing taxes, learning a new skill |

> **The critical problem:** System 2 thinks it's in charge. It's not. System 1 shapes most decisions before System 2 even gets involved.

## The Bias Catalog: What Goes Wrong and Why

### 1. Anchoring Effect

**What it is:** The first number you hear disproportionately influences your estimate, even if it's completely arbitrary.

**How it works:**
- Ask someone "Is the population of Chicago more or less than 5 million?" then ask them to estimate → answers cluster near 5M
- Ask another group with an anchor of 200,000 → answers cluster near 200K

**Where it gets you:**
- Salary negotiations (whoever names a number first sets the anchor)
- Real estate pricing (listing price anchors all offers)
- Poker: a large opening bet anchors what feels like a "reasonable" call
- Business: initial investor valuations anchor all future rounds

**Defense:** Always generate your own estimate BEFORE seeing anyone else's number. Be suspicious of any "starting point" someone gives you.

### 2. Availability Heuristic

**What it is:** You judge probability by how easily examples come to mind, not by actual frequency.

**How it works:**
- After seeing a plane crash on the news, you overestimate flying risk
- Vivid, emotional, recent events feel more likely than they are
- Things you can picture easily seem more common

**Where it gets you:**
- Overestimating rare dramatic risks (terrorism) while ignoring common ones (heart disease)
- Business: over-investing in solving problems you personally experienced
- Poker: overweighting memorable bad beats, underweighting boring winning sessions

**Defense:** Ask "Is this actually common, or just memorable?" Demand base rates.

### 3. Substitution

**What it is:** When faced with a hard question, System 1 quietly substitutes an easier one and answers that instead.

| Hard Question | System 1 Substitutes |
|---------------|---------------------|
| "Is this a good investment?" | "Do I like the founder?" |
| "How likely is this startup to succeed?" | "Does the narrative sound compelling?" |
| "Is this product worth $1,000?" | "How do I feel about this brand?" |
| "Should I call this bet?" | "Am I on a heater right now?" |

**Defense:** When making important decisions, write down the actual question you're trying to answer. Check: "Am I actually answering THIS question, or a different one?"

### 4. WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is)

**What it is:** System 1 builds the best possible story from available information — and never asks "What am I missing?"

**How it works:**
- You hear one side of a story and form a strong opinion
- The less you know, the more confident you feel (because the story is simpler and more coherent)
- System 1 suppresses ambiguity and doubt

**Where it gets you:**
- First impressions that resist updating (hiring, dating, business partnerships)
- Poker: constructing a "story" about an opponent's hand based on limited information
- Business: launching products based on your own experience without market research
- Investing: conviction based on a compelling narrative without checking disconfirming data

**Defense:** Actively ask: "What information am I missing? What would change my mind?"

### 5. Overconfidence

**What it is:** People's confidence in their judgments is systematically higher than their accuracy warrants.

**The data:**
- When people say they're "99% sure," they're wrong ~40% of the time
- Experts are only slightly better calibrated than laypeople
- Overconfidence increases with expertise (you get more confident faster than you get more accurate)

**Where it gets you:**
- Business plans that assume everything goes right
- Investment concentration ("I'm sure this one will work")
- Poker: playing too many hands because you overestimate your edge
- Underestimating project timelines (the Planning Fallacy — see below)

**Defense:** Use the pre-mortem technique. Before committing, ask: "It's one year from now and this failed completely. Why?"

### 6. Loss Aversion

**What it is:** Losses hurt roughly **2x more** than equivalent gains feel good. Losing $100 feels as bad as winning $200 feels good.

**Consequences:**
- People reject positive-expected-value bets because the potential loss looms larger
- Investors hold losing stocks too long (selling = realizing the loss) and sell winners too early (lock in the gain)
- Business: founders invest more in protecting existing revenue than in pursuing bigger new opportunities
- Poker: playing scared money — tightening up when losing, unable to make correct aggressive plays

**The formula:** For most people, a 50/50 bet must offer ~2.5x the potential loss to be accepted.

**Defense:** Evaluate decisions by expected value, not by how the loss scenario feels. Ask: "If I faced this decision 1,000 times, what's the best policy?"

### 7. The Planning Fallacy

**What it is:** People consistently underestimate time, costs, and risks of future actions while overestimating benefits.

**The data:**
- The average kitchen renovation exceeds budget by 50%+
- Software projects regularly take 2-3x longer than estimated
- Business founders routinely underestimate time-to-profitability by years

**Root cause:** People plan based on the best-case scenario (System 1's optimistic narrative) rather than base rates (what actually happens to similar projects).

**Defense:** Use **reference class forecasting**: find 10 similar projects/businesses/ventures and look at what actually happened to them. The outside view beats the inside view.

### 8. Sunk Cost Fallacy

**What it is:** Continuing to invest in something because of what you've already put in, rather than based on future expected value.

**Where it gets you:**
- Staying in a failing business because you've invested 2 years
- Continuing to watch a terrible movie because you paid for the ticket
- Poker: calling a losing bet because you've already put money in the pot
- Holding a losing stock because you'd have to "admit you were wrong"

**Defense:** Ask only: "Given where I am right now, what's the best move forward?" Past investment is irrelevant to future decisions.

### 9. Regression to the Mean

**What it is:** Extreme performance (good or bad) is likely followed by more average performance, purely from statistics — not from any causal change.

**Where it fools you:**
- A business has an incredible Q1 → you invest → Q2 is average (natural regression, not decline)
- A poker player runs bad for a week → changes their strategy → starts winning (probably would have anyway)
- Punishing someone after bad performance → they improve → you credit the punishment (it was just regression)

**Defense:** Before attributing cause, ask: "Would this outcome have naturally regressed toward the mean anyway?"

### 10. Narrative Fallacy

**What it is:** The irresistible human tendency to construct coherent stories from random events, then believe the story explains and predicts.

**Where it gets you:**
- Business retrospectives that find "causes" for success that were actually luck
- "This company succeeded because of X" (when 50 companies did X and failed)
- Poker: constructing elaborate reads on opponents based on 3 hands of data

**Defense:** Distinguish between stories that explain the past (easy, unreliable) and predictions that can be tested (hard, valuable).

## Prospect Theory: How Humans Actually Value Risk

Kahneman's Nobel Prize-winning framework. Replaces classical economics' "rational actor" model.

### The Four-Fold Pattern

| | High Probability | Low Probability |
|---|---|---|
| **Gains** | RISK AVERSE (prefer sure thing) — "Take the $900 instead of 95% chance of $1,000" | RISK SEEKING (buy lottery tickets) — "Pay $5 for a 0.001% chance at $10M" |
| **Losses** | RISK SEEKING (gamble to avoid certain loss) — "Risk losing $1,000 rather than accept certain $900 loss" | RISK AVERSE (overpay for insurance) — "Pay $50/month to avoid 0.01% chance of $100K loss" |

**Why this matters for business/poker/investing:**
- People systematically overpay for certainty and for protection against rare catastrophes
- People systematically gamble to avoid locking in losses
- Insurance companies and casinos exploit the bottom-right quadrant
- Good poker players exploit opponents stuck in the top-left quadrant (too eager to lock in small wins)

### The Value Function

```
     Gains
       |    /
       |   / ← Concave (diminishing sensitivity)
       |  /
       | /
-------+----------
      /|
     / |
    /  | ← Steeper slope (loss aversion ~2x)
   /   |
  Losses
```

- **Reference dependence:** People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, not absolute values
- **Diminishing sensitivity:** The difference between $100 and $200 feels bigger than between $1,100 and $1,200
- **Loss aversion:** The curve is steeper for losses than for gains (~2x)

## Expert Intuition: When to Trust Your Gut

Not all intuition is bad. Kahneman (with Gary Klein) identified two conditions where expert intuition is reliable:

### The Two Conditions for Valid Intuition

1. **High-validity environment:** Regular patterns exist and are learnable
   - Chess: yes (positions repeat, patterns are stable)
   - Poker: yes for hand reading, no for outcome prediction
   - Stock picking: mostly no (too random)
   - Firefighting: yes (smoke patterns, building behavior)

2. **Sufficient practice with feedback:** The expert has had many repetitions with quick, clear feedback
   - Experienced poker players reading opponents: valid (thousands of hands, immediate feedback)
   - Doctors diagnosing common conditions: valid
   - Political pundits predicting elections: invalid (too few reps, delayed/ambiguous feedback)
   - VCs picking startups: weak (small sample, long feedback loops, survivorship bias)

### When to Trust vs. Override Your Gut

| Trust Your Gut When | Override Your Gut When |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Environment has repeating patterns | Environment is fundamentally random |
| You have 10,000+ reps with feedback | You have limited experience in this domain |
| The decision is within your area of expertise | You're extrapolating outside your expertise |
| Time pressure requires fast action | You have time to analyze |
| The situation pattern-matches a category you've seen many times | The situation is novel or unprecedented |

## Practical Decision Frameworks

### The Pre-Mortem

Before committing to any major decision:
1. Imagine it's **one year from now** and the project/investment/decision **has failed completely**
2. Each person independently writes down **why it failed**
3. Collect all reasons and assess which are most plausible
4. Build mitigation plans for the top risks

**Why it works:** Overcomes groupthink and overconfidence by legitimizing doubt. People are surprisingly good at imagining causes of failure — they just don't do it unless asked.

### Reference Class Forecasting

For any estimate (time, cost, revenue, probability):
1. Find the **reference class** — similar projects/businesses/investments
2. Look at the **distribution of actual outcomes** in that class
3. Start your estimate from the **base rate**, not from your internal optimistic narrative
4. Adjust only modestly for special factors

**Example:** "Our app will launch in 3 months" → Find 20 similar app launches → Median was 7 months → Start from 7, adjust down only with strong evidence.

### The Mediating Assessments Protocol

For complex evaluations (hiring, investments, business decisions):
1. Define **4-6 independent dimensions** to evaluate (e.g., for hiring: technical skill, communication, culture fit, problem-solving, relevant experience)
2. Score each dimension **independently** on a scale (don't let one dimension contaminate another)
3. Only after scoring all dimensions, form your **overall judgment**
4. Weight the dimensions by importance

**Why it works:** Prevents the halo effect (one strong impression coloring everything else) and forces System 2 engagement.

### Expected Value Thinking

For decisions under uncertainty:
1. List all possible outcomes
2. Assign realistic probabilities (use base rates, not gut feelings)
3. Calculate: **EV = Σ (probability × outcome value)**
4. Choose the option with the highest expected value
5. Accept that any individual outcome may be bad — focus on being right over many decisions

**Critical insight:** A decision can be correct even if the outcome is bad. Judging decisions by outcomes (rather than process) is one of the most destructive biases — Kahneman calls this **outcome bias**.

## Application to Poker

Poker is one of the best real-world laboratories for Kahneman's ideas because it involves repeated decisions under uncertainty with fast feedback.

| Bias | Poker Manifestation | Counter |
|------|---------------------|---------|
| Anchoring | A big opening bet anchors "reasonable" calling range | Calculate pot odds independently before seeing bet size |
| Availability | You remember the time your aces got cracked, so you play them timidly | Track actual stats, not memorable hands |
| Loss aversion | You can't fold a big pot because losing it feels devastating | Evaluate each decision independently by EV |
| Sunk cost | "I already put $500 in, I have to call" | Chips already in the pot aren't yours anymore |
| Overconfidence | Overestimating your edge in a lineup | Honest session tracking and win-rate analysis |
| Tilt (System 1 hijack) | After a bad beat, System 1 floods with emotion and overrides System 2 | Pre-commit to stop-loss rules; take breaks |
| Narrative fallacy | "He always bluffs the river" based on 2 hands | Large sample sizes before forming reads |
| Regression to mean | Changing strategy after a downswing | Trust your long-run stats, not short-run results |

## Application to Business & Investing

| Bias | Business/Investing Manifestation | Counter |
|------|----------------------------------|---------|
| Planning fallacy | "We'll be profitable in 6 months" (takes 2 years) | Reference class: what % of similar startups hit profitability in 6 months? |
| Overconfidence | Investing everything in one idea because "I know this will work" | Diversify; pre-mortem; kill criteria |
| WYSIATI | Making business decisions based only on your experience | Customer research, market data, disconfirming evidence |
| Anchoring | First funding round valuation shapes all future expectations | Independent valuation before seeing any offers |
| Narrative fallacy | "Airbnb succeeded because of great design" (ignoring 100 failed copycats) | Survivorship bias check — what happened to the others? |
| Loss aversion | Refusing to pivot because it means admitting the original idea failed | Evaluate: "If I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?" |
| Sunk cost | Pouring money into a failing product because you've already spent $200K | Only future expected returns matter |
| Substitution | "Is this a good business?" → "Do I like doing this?" | Separate passion from unit economics analysis |

## The Two Selves

Kahneman's final major insight: you have two selves that evaluate experiences differently.

### Experiencing Self vs. Remembering Self

| | Experiencing Self | Remembering Self |
|---|---|---|
| **Evaluates** | Moment-to-moment experience | Memory of the experience |
| **Rule** | Cares about duration and intensity | Ignores duration, remembers peak + end |
| **Example** | A 2-week vacation vs. a 1-week vacation | Both remembered equally if the peak and ending were the same |

### Peak-End Rule

People judge an experience based on:
1. **The peak** — the most intense moment (good or bad)
2. **The end** — how it finished

Duration is almost completely ignored.

**Applications:**
- Customer experience: make the ending great (even if the middle was rough)
- Poker sessions: a big win at the end makes the whole session feel positive
- Job interviews: nail the last impression
- Product design: the unboxing and the last interaction matter most

## 10-Point Decision Quality Checklist

Before any major decision, run through this:

- [ ] **1. What question am I actually answering?** (Substitution check)
- [ ] **2. Am I anchored by someone else's number?** (Anchoring check)
- [ ] **3. Is this memorable or actually frequent?** (Availability check)
- [ ] **4. What information am I missing?** (WYSIATI check)
- [ ] **5. What's the base rate for this type of outcome?** (Reference class check)
- [ ] **6. Am I overconfident? What would a pre-mortem reveal?** (Overconfidence check)
- [ ] **7. Am I being influenced by sunk costs?** (Sunk cost check)
- [ ] **8. Am I in the gains or losses domain right now?** (Prospect theory check)
- [ ] **9. Is my gut valid here? (Repeating patterns + thousands of reps?)** (Expert intuition check)
- [ ] **10. What's the expected value if I run this decision 1,000 times?** (EV check)
