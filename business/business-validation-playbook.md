A step-by-step guide to validating a business idea before you invest significant time and money.

## Phase 1: Problem Discovery

### Goal: Find a real problem worth solving

**Start with yourself:**
- What daily frustrations do you just live with?
- What have you assumed "is just the way the world is"?
- What annoyances have you been hoping someone else would solve?

**Then observe the world:**
- Go out with your eyes open, looking for problems, annoyances, frictions
- Notice things that are suboptimal — even slightly
- Pay attention to what people DO, not what they SAY
- Watch how people use existing products and services
- Notice the subtle behaviors they might not even be aware of

### The Entrepreneur's Distinction

> "The difference between entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs is the entrepreneur says: and I'm going to be the one that solves that problem."

Everyone notices problems. Entrepreneurs decide to fix them.

## Phase 2: Problem Validation

### Goal: Confirm this is a real, widespread, paying problem

### Questions to Answer

1. **Is this a need that only you have, or do many people have it?**
2. **How urgent and pressing is the need?**
3. **Are people already spending money to solve this problem?** (Best possible signal)
4. **How much would people pay to solve it?**

### Methods

| Method | Description | Signal Strength |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| Ask friends/family | "Would you buy this?" | Very weak (means nothing) |
| Talk to target customers | Qualitative interviews | Medium |
| Survey potential market | Quantitative data on preferences | Medium |
| Check for existing competitors | People already paying to solve this | Strong |
| Ask for pre-orders | Credit cards swiped before product exists | Very strong |
| Letters of intent | B2B commitment to purchase | Very strong |

### The Credit Card Test

> "Is this enough of a problem for you to pay money to solve it?"

- Not "do you like this idea?"
- Not "would you use this?"
- **"Will you pay for this right now?"**

Use mechanisms like:
- Kickstarter/crowdfunding campaigns
- Pre-order landing pages
- Letters of intent (B2B)
- Early access pricing

### Red Flags

- Everyone says they love it but no one will pre-order
- Only friends and family are enthusiastic
- No competitors exist in the space (market may not exist)
- The problem is real but not painful enough to pay for

### Green Flags

- People are already spending money on inferior solutions
- Multiple competitors exist and are profitable
- Target customers immediately say "I need this"
- Pre-orders come in with real payment information
- Potential customers describe the problem without prompting

## Phase 3: Solution Design

### Goal: Create a minimum viable version of your solution

### Apply Gall's Law

> "Any complex system that works evolved from a simpler system that worked."

- Start with the simplest possible version
- Don't build the multinational distribution version yet
- Every additional feature must earn its place
- Complexity isn't value

### Make Trade-offs

The perfect product would deliver everything the customer wants for free. That's impossible. So:

1. List everything customers want from a product in this category
2. Identify which 1-2 dimensions matter most to your target customers
3. Turn those dials to 9, 10, 11
4. Accept trade-offs on other dimensions

### Choose Your Form of Value

Josh Kaufman identifies 12 forms of value delivery. Your solution will take one or more of these forms:

- Product
- Service
- Shared resource
- Subscription
- Resale
- Lease
- Agency
- Audience aggregation
- Loan
- Option
- Insurance
- Capital

### Prototype and Iterate

1. Create a rough prototype or minimum viable product
2. Get it in front of potential customers as quickly as possible
3. Collect feedback (what works, what doesn't)
4. Iterate based on real data, not assumptions

## Phase 4: Small-Scale Test

### Goal: Validate the full business model with real transactions

### First Batch Strategy

- Do a short production run to mitigate risk
- Get your initial feedback from first-run customers
- They will have strong opinions about what works and what doesn't
- Use this to refine before scaling

### Testing the Five Parts

| Part | What to Test | How |
|------|-------------|-----|
| Value Creation | Does the product/service actually solve the problem? | Customer feedback, usage data |
| Marketing | Can you attract attention from the right people? | Small ad spend, content, outreach |
| Sales | Will people actually pay the price you're asking? | Conversion rates, objection tracking |
| Value Delivery | Can you fulfill orders reliably? | Delivery times, quality consistency |
| Finance | Does the math work? | Revenue vs. costs at small scale |

### Key Metrics to Track

- **Customer acquisition cost:** How much does it cost to get one customer?
- **Conversion rate:** What percentage of interested people actually buy?
- **Customer satisfaction:** Are people happy with what they received?
- **Repeat purchase rate:** Do customers come back?
- **Unit economics:** Revenue per sale minus all costs per sale

## Phase 5: The Sustainability Decision

### Goal: Determine if this is worth pursuing long-term

### The Sufficiency Question

It's not just "is this profitable?" It's:

> "Is it enough to make all of the time, energy, and effort worthwhile?"

### Calculate Your Sustainability Number

Based on your life and lifestyle:
- What monthly income do you need to make this venture sustainable?
- What are your personal fixed costs?
- How much runway do you have?
- At what point does this replace or supplement your current income?

### Decision Framework

| Scenario | Action |
|----------|--------|
| Pre-orders strong, unit economics work, customers happy | Scale up gradually |
| Some interest but unit economics don't work yet | Adjust pricing, reduce costs, or pivot approach |
| Customers happy but market too small | Consider adjacent markets or expanded offerings |
| No one is buying despite good marketing | Re-examine value proposition — you may be solving the wrong problem |
| Making money but it's not enough for the time invested | Either find ways to increase margins/volume or move on |

## The Experimentation Framework

### The Explore/Exploit Trade-off

Think of it like walking into a casino with 100 slot machines, each with different payoffs:

1. **Explore:** Try many different approaches, collect information
2. **Analyze:** Look for patterns — some things clearly work better than others
3. **Exploit:** Do the best-performing things more often
4. **Keep exploring:** Never stop trying new things — just reduce the percentage over time

### Applied to Business

| Phase | Explore | Exploit |
|-------|---------|---------|
| Product | Try 10 different scents | Focus on the 2-3 that sell best |
| Marketing | Test multiple channels and messages | Double down on what converts |
| Sales | Try different pricing and packaging | Standardize what works |
| Distribution | Test online, retail, wholesale | Commit to strongest channels |

### The Critical Rule

> Experimentation without feedback collection is wasted time.

For every experiment:
1. Define what you're testing
2. Define how you'll measure results
3. Run the experiment
4. Collect the data
5. Make a decision based on what you learned

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Playing Business Instead of Doing Business

| Don't Start With | Start With |
|------------------|------------|
| Picking a logo | Talking to potential customers |
| Designing business cards | Getting pre-orders |
| Setting up a fancy website | Validating the problem is real |
| Registering trademarks | Making a small batch and selling it |
| Spending 2-3 years building | Getting to first sale as fast as possible |

### 2. The Big Launch Trap

- Spending years building up to one launch day
- Everything riding on a single moment
- Raising $70K before any validation
- No customer feedback loop until it's too late

**Instead:** Start small, test constantly, iterate quickly.

### 3. Over-Engineering the Product

- Adding features that don't serve core customer needs
- Confusing complexity with value
- Changing the product's frame by adding unrelated features
- Not understanding that more isn't more

### 4. Ignoring Competition

- Assuming you need a totally novel idea
- Not buying and studying competitor products
- Not understanding why existing solutions succeed or fail
- Missing the opportunity to learn from the market for free

### 5. Building on Opinions Instead of Data

- Relying on friends' and family's enthusiasm
- Not asking for actual purchase commitments
- Ignoring behavioral signals in favor of verbal feedback
- Making big bets without small-scale testing first

## Quick Validation Checklist

- [ ] I can describe the problem in one sentence
- [ ] I've talked to 10+ people who have this problem (not friends/family)
- [ ] I can identify people already spending money on inferior solutions
- [ ] I've gotten at least a few pre-orders or purchase commitments
- [ ] I've studied competitors and understand what they do well and poorly
- [ ] My solution starts simple (Gall's Law)
- [ ] I've chosen 1-2 dimensions to focus on and turn up to 11
- [ ] I've done a small-scale test with real customers
- [ ] The unit economics work (revenue per sale > cost per sale)
- [ ] I know my sustainability number and have a path to reach it
